# Creative research playbook

How to research a brand for a UGC ad, what to pull out, and the brief to hand back.

## Contents
1. Where to look
2. What to extract
3. Turning findings into angles
4. The Creative Research Brief template

## 1. Where to look

Work from the most direct sources outward. Fetch pages rather than relying on search
snippets, which are too short to find customer language in.

**The brand's own channels**
- The product page: what it is, price, variants, the offer (bundle, discount,
  free shipping, guarantee), the claims it makes, the photos.
- The homepage and About page: positioning, founder story, tone of voice.
- FAQ and shipping/returns pages: the objections the brand already knows about.
- The brand's social profiles (TikTok, Instagram, YouTube) if reachable: who appears
  in their content, the style, which posts got attention.

**Customer voice (the most valuable part)**
- Reviews on the product page, Amazon, Trustpilot, Google, app stores — whichever
  apply. Read the 5-star reviews for desires and results, and the 2–3 star reviews
  for objections and disappointments. 1-star reviews are often shipping complaints;
  skim them for real product problems.
- Reddit threads (search `<product or category> reddit`), forums, YouTube comments,
  TikTok comments: how people talk about the problem when no brand is listening.
- If the brand has few reviews, research the category instead: what do people say
  about the problem and about competing products?

**Competitors and the category**
- Search for 2–4 direct competitors and skim how their pages and ads talk.
- Ad libraries (Meta Ad Library, TikTok Creative Center) show live ads, but they
  often need a browser and sign-in. Use them if you can reach them; otherwise note
  the category's common angles from competitor pages and reviews. Don't pretend
  you saw ads you couldn't open.
- Look for what everyone says (the clichés to avoid) and what nobody says (a gap).

## 2. What to extract

Keep notes as you go; this is what the brief is built from.

- **Product facts**: what it is, what it does, how it's used, price, offer.
- **Audience**: who buys (age range, situation, identity), and their trigger moment
  (what happened that made them look for this).
- **Pains**: the problem in the customer's words, including the emotional side
  ("embarrassed", "tired of", "gave up on").
- **Failed alternatives**: what they tried before and why it didn't work. This is
  often the strongest hook material.
- **Desires and results**: what changed for them, as specifically as reviews state it.
- **Objections**: price, doubt it works, fit/size, taste, setup effort, trust in the
  brand. Note how the brand answers each (guarantee, free returns, trial).
- **Proof**: rating and review count, specific reported results, press, awards,
  certifications, years in business, units sold — only what's stated somewhere you
  can point to.
- **Voice**: playful / clinical / premium / no-nonsense; words the brand uses.
- **Visual cues**: colours, settings, the kind of people in their content, product
  moments reviewers mention (the texture, the click, the unboxing).
- **Compliance flags**: regulated category, medical or financial claims, claims you
  couldn't verify.

Collect 5–10 verbatim customer phrases, each short (a few words to a sentence) with
where it came from. They feed the hook and the script.

## 3. Turning findings into angles

An angle answers: *for whom, which problem or desire, what proof, what hook.* Build
each from a different part of the research so the options are genuinely different:

- **Pain-led** — the sharpest pain plus the failed alternative.
  Hook pattern: "I tried X, Y and Z before I found this."
- **Desire/result-led** — the most specific result reviewers report.
  Hook pattern: "Three weeks ago I [specific before-state]."
- **Objection-led** — the most common doubt, faced head-on.
  Hook pattern: "I was sure this was another overpriced [category]."
- **Story-led** — a relatable situation from the reviews, product arrives naturally.
- **Contrarian** — the gap competitors leave; say what the category won't.

Hooks that work in UGC are specific, spoken naturally, and promise something within
the first second or two. Avoid opening with the brand name or a question nobody
asked ("Do you struggle with...?").

Recommend the angle with the strongest real proof behind it. A modest claim backed
by many reviews beats a dramatic one backed by nothing.

## 4. The Creative Research Brief template

Keep it to about one screen. Short quotes only, with the source named.

```markdown
# Creative research: <Brand> — <Product>

**Product:** <what it is and does, one or two sentences> · <price / offer>

**Who buys it:** <specific audience and trigger moment>

**What they say** (customer language)
- "<short quote>" — <source>
- "<short quote>" — <source>
- "<short quote>" — <source>

**Pains & failed alternatives:** <2–3 bullets>

**Objections & how they're answered:** <2–3 bullets>

**Proof we can use:** <rating, review count, specific results, guarantees — sourced>

**Category & competitors:** <how others advertise; the gap>

**Brand voice & look:** <tone, visual style, who appears in their content>

**Flags:** <unverified claims kept out, regulated category notes; "none" if none>

## Angles
1. **<Name>** (recommended) — <format>, <funnel stage>. <One-sentence idea.>
   Hook: "<hook line>"
2. **<Name>** — <format>, <funnel stage>. <Idea.> Hook: "<hook line>"
3. **<Name>** — <format>, <funnel stage>. <Idea.> Hook: "<hook line>"

Sources: <links>
```

If the client has a filesystem (Claude Code, Cursor, a desktop agent), also save the
brief as `creative-research-<brand>.md` so variations can reuse it later.
